# 3. Do not waste energy

This section counts energy and time only. It looks at four things: how much attention you have available each day, how fast you react, how many mistakes you make, and how long it takes you to get back after an interruption. All the numbers were measured directly, either in experiments or from records kept by tracking a group of people over time (cohort studies). These numbers are not converted into lifespan, and they are not converted into death rates. Most entries in this section are graded B. That is simply how this field is, and it does not mean the advice does not hold. §3.19 (treating the police, the doctor and the clerk as people doing a job by rules) is about what to expect when you deal with police, doctors and bank clerks, and it cites regulations rather than experiments, so it is graded C. §3.15 (treat a pessimistic thought as a symptom) and §3.22 (treat "other people demand that I be perfect" as a symptom) cite figures on death rates and suicide risk. Those are measured on a different lens from the rest of this section, so do not compare them side by side with the others.

The entries in this section fall into the blocks below by theme, with entry numbers in brackets.

**Attention while you work**: turn off non-essential notifications (§3.1), handle messages in batches a few times a day (§3.5), keep out even a few seconds of interruption (§3.6), do one thing at a time (§3.7), use earplugs in an open-plan office (§3.12), do not keep pushing your weekly hours up (§3.13).

**Sleep and late nights**: keep a fixed wake-up time (§3.2), get 7 to 8 hours a night (§3.3), no caffeine after two in the afternoon (§3.4), no screens in the hour before bed (§3.8), go to bed when it is time (§3.9), make no major decision late at night (§3.10), nap 10 minutes when you get sleepy in the afternoon (§3.11).

**Thoughts in your head**: switch to something you do with your hands when you go over something again and again (§3.14), treat a pessimistic thought as a symptom (§3.15), halve your estimate when you think you made a fool of yourself (§3.21), treat "other people demand that I be perfect" as a symptom (§3.22).

**Anger and low mood**: leave the room when you are angry (§3.17), the first few things to do when your mood is low (§3.18), do not vent anger by smashing things (§3.23), write down what is weighing on you for several days in a row (§3.24).

**People and social media**: learn to turn down a request you do not want (§3.16), treat the police and doctors as people doing a job by rules (§3.19), set a limit on apps that show you how your peers are doing (§3.20).

**Pain in your body**: take an ibuprofen-type painkiller for period pain (§3.25).

### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications, and keep your phone out of sight while you work
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You spend a few minutes changing a setting, and it takes almost no willpower.
- In plain words: When your phone goes off, your score on an attention task drops, even if you did not look at it and did not touch it. It drops by about as much as if you had actually taken the call or sent the text. There is another situation too: the phone is just sitting on the desk, you hold off from looking at it, and the capacity your brain has available still goes down. The more you cannot be without your phone, the more it drops.
- Benefit: Simply receiving a notification, without looking at it or touching it, made scores on an attention task clearly worse. The size of that drop is comparable to actually taking or making a call, or sending a text. This was done in a laboratory, and the participants were university students. Two other experiments show that even when people successfully held off from looking at the phone, having their own phone next to them reduced the capacity their brain had available. People with strong phone dependence dropped the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>；Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: These are all single tasks done in a laboratory. Nobody has measured what builds up when it goes on day after day. If your job genuinely requires you to be reachable at any time, you can leave notifications on for a handful of contacts only.

### 2. Keep your wake-up time fixed, at the weekend as well
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You have to sleep in less at the weekend, which takes a bit of willpower.
- In plain words: Among the fifth of people with the most irregular pattern, the body clock ran about 2.5 hours later than in the most regular fifth. That amounts to living in a permanent time difference. The more regular a student's pattern was, the better their academic results.
- Benefit: 61 university students kept records for 30 days. In the fifth with the most irregular pattern, the body clock (the circadian rhythm) ran about 2.5 hours later than in the most regular fifth. Specifically, melatonin started being released at 00:08 against 21:32. The stretch when they were sleepiest and most wanted to sleep also came about 1.8 hours later. The more regular the pattern, the better the academic results (correlation coefficient r = 0.37).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: There is only this one study, with few people and no control group. The researchers reason that the difference in the body clock comes mainly from how much light each person gets each day. So a fixed wake-up time only works if you also get light in the morning. For how to make up for the odd late night, see §2.38 (sleep it back the next night after a late night): the way to do it is to go to bed early that evening, not to sleep until noon the next day.

### 3. Get 7 to 8 hours of sleep a night, and do not treat 6 hours as enough
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You have to set aside 1 to 2 more hours a day for sleep. Going to bed earlier takes a bit of willpower.
- In plain words: Sleep only 6 hours a night for two weeks in a row and your brain's performance drops to about the level of two days and nights without closing your eyes. The trouble is that you cannot feel it. Your own rating of how sleepy you are rises in the first few days and then stops rising, and people on 6 hours rate themselves as sleepy as people on 4 hours. So "6 hours is enough for me" is itself a symptom of not getting enough sleep.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours a night for 14 days in a row. Their mental performance declined cumulatively, down to about the level of 2 nights with no sleep at all. Their own rating of how sleepy they were rose only in the first few days and then barely increased further. There was no difference in self-rated sleepiness between the 6-hour group and the 4-hour group. This was a controlled trial with people split into two groups at random (an RCT). Separately, for short periods without sleep (acute sleep deprivation, no more than 48 hours), the effect size on simple attention lapses was g = -0.776, and on reasoning accuracy g = -0.125. That part comes from pooling 70 studies together.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>；Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: "I do not feel sleepy" is not evidence that you slept enough. People who sleep 6 hours long term have only got used to the feeling, and their performance has not kept up. The part that pools several studies is about going a whole night without sleep. For "long-term short sleep" there is only one randomised controlled trial behind it. How long you sleep and your risk of dying is counted separately, see §2.13 (about 7 hours of sleep a night): that one counts death rates, and the 7-hour band carries the lowest risk.

### 4. Do not touch caffeine after two in the afternoon
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. If you are used to coffee in the afternoon, holding off takes a bit of willpower.
- In plain words: Drink a large coffee (400 mg) 6 hours before bed and instruments measure more than 1 hour less sleep that night. But your own sleep diary shows no difference at all. An ordinary cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to leave sleep unaffected. If you go to bed at eleven, two in the afternoon is the cut-off.
- Benefit: Taking 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed cut instrument-measured total sleep time by 1.1 to 1.2 hours compared with a placebo. The placebo is a control drink with no caffeine in it. But the participants' own sleep diaries reported no difference, so they could not tell. Pooling several studies together (a meta-analysis): caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 minutes on average. The share of time in bed actually spent asleep (sleep efficiency) fell by 7%. A cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to leave sleep unaffected.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>；Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: Only one laboratory has run the Drake study, and the 400 mg it used is on the large side, roughly two or three cups of coffee. For people who metabolise caffeine slowly, the cut-off has to be earlier still. What you get back from cutting caffeine is sleep time, and it adds to §3.3 (get 7 to 8 hours of sleep a night).

### 5. Change email and messages to a few fixed batches a day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You spend a little time telling your colleagues how often you reply. Holding off from checking email all the time takes a bit of willpower.
- In plain words: The same people were compared over two weeks. In the week when they were limited to checking email 3 times a day, their everyday stress was clearly lower, and they sent and received just as many emails. With no limit, people checked 15.5 times a day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, with the same people compared before and after across two weeks (each person as their own control). In the week limited to checking email 3 times a day, everyday stress was clearly lower than in the week with no limit on the number of checks (effect size Cohen's d = 0.37). The number of emails received and replied to did not differ between the two weeks. At the start, with no limit, people checked email 15.5 times a day on average.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: There is only one study, the effect is medium to small, and what it measured is stress, not work done. Nobody has studied instant messaging directly, so that part is only inferred here.

### 6. When you are doing work that needs continuous thought, keep out even an interruption of a few seconds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Shut the door, put headphones on, or hang up a "do not disturb" sign.
- In plain words: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubled the chance of an error on the step that followed. An interruption of 4.4 seconds tripled it. Observation in the field also found that it took 25 minutes on average to get back to the interrupted task, with two other things coming in between. People who were interrupted worked faster, but their stress, frustration and effort were all higher.
- Benefit: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubled the step error rate on a task done one step at a time in order (a sequential task). An interruption of 4.4 seconds tripled it. This was done in a laboratory. Another study observed 24 office workers (information workers) in the field. They switched work topic every 11 minutes on average. 57% of work topics were interrupted. When they returned to the interrupted task that day, it took 25 minutes 26 seconds on average. 2.26 other things came in between. There is also a 48-person experiment: people who were interrupted worked faster, but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>；Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>；Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: That field study split interruptions into two kinds: ones where somebody comes to you, and ones you open yourself. Shutting the door only keeps out the first kind. For the second kind you need §3.1 (turn off non-essential notifications) and §3.5 (handle messages in batches) alongside it.

### 7. Do one thing at a time, and do not answer messages during a meeting
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Holding off from answering messages during a meeting takes a bit of willpower.
- In plain words: As soon as you switch tasks, you react more slowly and you make more mistakes. People who often have several screens going at once have not trained up any ability to do several things at once, and in fact they do worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After a task switch, people reacted clearly more slowly and made more mistakes. That part comes from a review of several studies. Another laboratory study had university students as participants. People who often use several kinds of media at once (heavy media multitaskers) are more easily pulled away by irrelevant things and irrelevant memories. They in fact did worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>；Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: How much extra switching back and forth costs varies a lot with the work, so no single figure is given here. The Ophir study compared people at one point in time, so it cannot tell apart "multitasking makes attention worse" from "people with worse attention multitask more".

### 8. Do not look at a light-emitting screen in the hour before bed; if you must read, read paper or an e-ink screen
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Putting the phone down an hour before bed means fighting habit for the long run, so it takes willpower.
- In plain words: Reading a light-emitting screen before bed meant falling asleep about 10 minutes later than reading a paper book. Melatonin was suppressed by 55%. The next day the body clock ran more than 1.5 hours later, people were sleepier in the morning, and took longer to wake up properly.
- Benefit: In a laboratory, the same people did both conditions in turn (a crossover trial). Reading a light-emitting e-reader before bed meant falling asleep about 10 minutes slower than reading a paper book (25.7 minutes against 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin was suppressed by 55%. The next day the body clock (the circadian rhythm) was delayed by more than 1.5 hours, with melatonin starting to be released at 22:31 against 21:01. The next morning people were sleepier and took longer to become fully alert.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: There were few people, and conditions were tightly controlled in a laboratory. At everyday phone brightness and everyday length of use, the effect may not be this large. How much good a phone's night mode does was not measured in this study.

### 9. Go to bed when it is time, and do not stay up for games, short videos or pornography
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is fighting the "one more episode" habit for the long run. Keeping the phone out of the bedroom takes the least effort.
- In plain words: In 2025 people in China slept 6.97 hours a night on average, and fell asleep at 0:10 on average. People who use a phone or tablet before bed are more than twice as likely not to get enough sleep, and 2.7 times as likely to be sleepy during the day. When the device is merely in the bedroom and not used, the figures are about the same. So the least troublesome approach is to keep the phone out of the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025 residents of China slept an average of 6.97 hours at night and fell asleep at 0:10 on average (Chinese Sleep Research Society). People who used a portable device before bed were about 2.17 times as likely not to get enough sleep (OR 2.17). Poor sleep quality was about 1.46 times as likely (OR 1.46). Daytime sleepiness was about 2.72 times as likely (OR 2.72). When the device was merely in the bedroom and not used, the figures were about the same, around 1.79, 1.53 and 2.27 times (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27). That part comes from pooling 20 surveys taken at one point in time, covering 125198 children and adolescents. Another survey at one point in time: among 844 adults aged 18 to 94, those who still used their phone after lights out took longer to fall asleep, spent a lower share of their time in bed actually asleep, and were more fatigued during the day.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>；Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>；中国睡眠研究会 (2026). 2026 中国睡眠健康研究白皮书（福建省卫健委转载健康报报道）. <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>；Grubbs JB 等 (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>（one of the two sides of the dispute in the notes）；Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>（the other side of the dispute in the notes）
- Notes: Disputed. For the cost of staying up late in itself, see §3.3 (get 7 to 8 hours of sleep a night). What effect pornography has on an ordinary person's energy and mental capacity is not something current evidence can pin on the content. One analysis pooling several studies holds that so-called "pornography problems" come more from a mismatch between a person's beliefs and their own behaviour than from the content itself. Another study pooled data on 280 men. The result was no relationship between how long people watched and erectile function during sex with a partner. This entry is only about it eating into your sleep time. For the two figures 6.97 hours and 0:10, the Chinese Sleep Research Society has not posted a release on its own website (checked 2026-09), so the report in Health News reposted by the Fujian Provincial Health Commission is the source used.

### 10. Make no major decision and send no important message late at night
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You only put things off until after you have slept.
- In plain words: When people are well rested, they quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After two nights in a row without sleep, they picked the high-risk ones again and again, and the older they were the more clearly this showed. Nobody has measured how much a few hours' less sleep matters, but it is still better to put an important decision off until after you have slept.
- Benefit: 34 healthy people did the Iowa Gambling Task after 49.5 hours without sleep. This is an experiment that measures risky decision-making. When well rested, they quickly learned to avoid the high-risk options. After losing sleep, they chose the high-risk options more often instead. Older people were affected more. This is one experiment, and there is only this one study.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: What the study looked at is the extreme case of two nights in a row without sleep. What happens to an ordinary person who stays up until two in the morning has not been measured directly. The "send no message" half is the author's experience (grade C), and sending it the next day is soon enough.

### 11. Nap 10 minutes when you get sleepy in the afternoon, not half an hour
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. It takes 10 minutes, plus somewhere you can lie down or lean forward on a desk.
- In plain words: A 10-minute nap picks you up on the spot, and the effect holds for two and a half hours. A 5-minute nap does basically nothing. A 20-minute nap only takes effect half an hour later. After a 30-minute nap you come round in a fog, and during that stretch you perform worse than if you had not slept.
- Benefit: After a night of not enough sleep, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, energy and mental performance, and some of the benefit lasted beyond 155 minutes. A 5-minute nap gave almost no benefit. The benefit of a 20-minute nap only appeared after 35 minutes. After a 30-minute nap there is first a stretch of sleep inertia: performance just after waking is worse instead, and improves only after that.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: There is only one laboratory study, and the participants were all healthy young people. If you cannot fall asleep, do not force it. Whether lying with your eyes shut for 10 minutes does any good has no research behind it. Napping for more than an hour is instead linked to higher death rates and higher coronary heart disease risk, see §2.37 (keep naps within half an hour).

### 12. In an open-plan office, use earplugs or find a quiet room for work that needs memory
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Earplugs cost a few tens of yuan. Booking a quiet meeting room takes a little time.
- In plain words: When background noise went up from 39 decibels to 51 decibels, people remembered fewer words, felt more tired and were less willing to work. During a break, watching a nature film with sound brought energy back better than going on listening to office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open-plan office. When background noise rose from 39 decibels to 51 decibels (dB, LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves as more fatigued, and were less willing to work. During a break, watching a nature film with sound brought their energy ratings back better than listening to river sounds alone or to office noise.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: There is only one laboratory study. The most disruptive part of noise is speech you can make out the words of. White-noise headphones help with that, but this study did not test headphones.

### 13. Do not keep pushing your weekly hours up: past about 49 hours, each extra hour produces less and less
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: You may get less overtime pay. You may also have to put effort into talking it through with your manager.
- In plain words: Records from British munitions factories in the First World War show that up to 49 hours a week, output rose in step with hours worked. Past 49 hours, each extra hour produced less and less. A 70-hour week produced almost the same as a 56-hour week, so those extra 14 hours were worked for nothing.
- Benefit: Output records for workers in British munitions factories during the First World War: up to 49 hours a week, output was proportional to hours worked. Past 49 hours, output started to grow more slowly. It reached its maximum at about 63 hours. Output at 70 hours was almost no different from output at 56 hours, so the extra 14 hours amounted to working for nothing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: The data come from factories a hundred years ago, the work was physical, and the workers were mostly women. That threshold may not be the same once you move it to mental work, and the author himself says it may be higher or lower for other kinds of job. What is reliable is the direction: past some point, working more produces almost nothing more. The specific figure of 49 is not reliable.

### 14. When you notice yourself going over the same bad thing again and again, switch to something you do with your hands
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is first noticing that you are going over it again and again (this is called rumination), and the noticing itself takes practice.
- In plain words: Going over the same bad thing again and again will not produce an answer. It only deepens depression, nails negative thoughts down harder, leaves you less able to solve problems and less able to actually act, and it wears out the people around you who are willing to listen. When you notice you are going round in circles, go and do something with your hands.
- Benefit: A review of several studies says that going over the same bad thing again and again (ruminative thinking) deepens depression. It also reinforces negative thinking and weakens your ability to solve problems. It gets in the way of acting in practice, and it wears out the people around you who are willing to listen. It is correlated with anxiety, binge eating, heavy drinking and self-harm.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: The review gives only a direction, with no figures. "Switch to something else" is the author's own practice. All the literature can support is one sentence: diverting your attention is better than going on circling in the same place. As for what to switch to, nobody has compared the options.

### 15. Treat a thought like "things are bound to get worse" as a symptom, not as a fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is treating a thought like this as a symptom rather than as a fact.
- In plain words: In a twin study that followed people for 20 years, each step up in pessimism came with about a 13% higher chance of dying over that period, and about a 20% higher chance of dying of cardiovascular disease. The other way round, a high optimism score brought no matching benefit. So what you have to deal with is pessimism, not "not being positive enough".
- Benefit: 2978 Australian twins aged over 50 were followed for an average of 20 years, during which 1068 of them died. For each 1 standard deviation higher on the pessimism scale, the risk of dying over that period was about 13% higher (hazard ratio 1.134). The confidence range for that figure is 95% CI 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵. Cardiovascular death was about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045–1.368, P=0.0093). There was no relationship with cancer death. Optimism scores also showed no clear association with death rates.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Disputed. This is observation only, with no groups assigned. Some will say that being ill came first and the pessimism followed, and the authors answer that if that were so, cancer death should have been associated too. But there may still be factors nobody measured. Optimism has no protective effect, so you do not have to force yourself to "think positive". What to do is stop treating a pessimistic expectation as an accurate prediction of the future.

### 16. Cut back the relationships that drain you, and learn to turn down a request you do not want
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. In the short run there will be social friction, and you may lose some relationships. Saying no out loud takes a fair amount of willpower.
- In plain words: The harm one person who drains you does is larger, and more consistent, than the good one person who supports you brings. So subtract first, then add.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89: the association between unpleasant interactions and psychological well-being was more consistent, and stronger, than the association with pleasant interactions. On this result, the harm done by one person who drains you is greater than the good brought by one person who supports you.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: The people surveyed were a particular group, and it was a one-off questionnaire, so you cannot see which came first. What it measured is well-being, not energy. Putting it in this section is the author's inference. There is no objective standard for which relationships count as "draining", so you can only judge for yourself.

### 17. Leave the room when you are angry, and treat the other person as weather rather than as an enemy
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. In the moment you have to hold off from answering back, turn around and walk away, and decide afterwards whether to deal with it. This takes some practice.
- In plain words: Putting a different reading on what just happened, or standing in the other person's position for a moment, both genuinely bring the feeling down, and the second works better. Pushing the feeling down so it does not show has no effect. Also, people who punished someone who had wronged them went over the incident for longer afterwards instead; people who did not retaliate moved on faster. People overestimate in advance how satisfying revenge will be.
- Benefit: Pooling 306 experimental comparisons together (a meta-analysis): putting a different reading on what happened (cognitive reappraisal) had an effect size on feelings of d = 0.36. Looking at it from the other person's position was d = 0.45. Diverting attention was d = 0.27. Pushing the feeling down did not work (d = -0.04). Three other experiments show that people who punished someone who had wronged them went over that person for longer afterwards instead, while people who did not punish them "moved on" faster. People overestimated in advance how satisfying revenge would be.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>；Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Notes: The part pooling several studies measured the intensity of the feeling, not energy, and the effect was small to medium. The revenge part rests on one set of experiments, which is why it is graded B. The "empty boat" is a parable from 《山木》 in the Zhuangzi: an empty boat drifts into you and you are not angry, but if there is someone on board you are, and yet the bump is the same. That is an analogy, not evidence. If you have really lost something, take the legal routes in section 8 and section 9, which are much cheaper than going off at the time.

### 18. When your mood is low, do the few things with the best return first: get moving, get sunlight, sleep on schedule, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing, or very little. Brisk walking, jogging or strength training a few times a week. Going outside in the daytime for sunlight. Settling into a fixed routine. Making a phone call when you cannot see a way out.
- In plain words: For depression, brisk walking and jogging work best, and the harder the effort the more effective it is, with the detail in §22.6 (walk or run when your mood is bad). Light therapy is strongly effective for seasonal depression, and effective for depression that is not seasonal too. Online cognitive behavioural self-help courses work, and when symptoms are heavier it is better to have someone guiding you through. If you cannot see a way out, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Pooling 218 randomised controlled trials covering 14170 people (a network meta-analysis). The control groups were people doing other activities. Compared with them, walking or jogging had an effect size on depression of g = -0.62 (95% confidence interval -0.80 to -0.45). Yoga was -0.55 and strength training -0.49. The harder the effort, the better the effect. Pooling several light therapy studies together: the effect size for seasonal depression was 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08), and for non-seasonal depression 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). There is also an analysis that pooled the individual patient data from each study. It looked at internet-based cognitive behavioural therapy (online self-help courses). Both with a guide and without a guide, it did better than the control groups. At moderate to severe levels (PHQ-9 above 9 points), having a guide did better. 12356 is the national psychological assistance hotline, open no fewer than 18 hours a day.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Noetel M 等 (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>；Golden RN 等 (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. - Notes: These are all supporting measures for mild to moderate depression. If it is moderate to severe, or there are suicidal thoughts, you need medical care, and call 12356 first, see §1.25 (depression or suicidal thoughts). Most of the exercise trials had few people, and they cannot hide who is exercising the way a drug trial can. The light therapy research only runs to 2003, and had few people as well. "Sleep on schedule" and "talk to someone" are the author's experience, with no separate literature behind them. Do not turn "get moving" into a demand on someone who is depressed, because ten minutes of walking counts.

### 19. Treat the police officer, the doctor and the clerk as people doing a job by rules, not as a role: what moves things along is paperwork and deadlines, not feelings
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. What changes is your expectations and the order you do things in. The first time, you have to hold off from the urge to argue your case until the other person agrees.
- In plain words: The police officer, the doctor and the clerk are all people working to the rules of a post, governed by paperwork, deadlines and oversight. Whether their attitude is good has basically nothing to do with whether your matter gets handled, or how many days it takes. So get the receipt, note the deadlines, and appeal through the channels, rather than trying to argue your case until the other person agrees.
- Benefit: Change what you do when you deal with an institution: instead of pleading, getting angry and repeating your grievance over and over, get the paperwork, note the deadlines, and appeal through the channels. What you save is the time spent on repeated communication and on going over it again and again. This is a one-off change of expectations, not a daily time saving. What you can check against is the deadlines themselves, which are all written down in the documents. For a public security administration case, counting from the day it is accepted, the handling period is no more than 30 days; for a case that is serious and complex, it can be extended by 30 days with the approval of the public security organ one level up. Where there is a complainant and a decision is made not to open a case, the notice of refusal to open a case has to be delivered within 3 days. The complainant can apply for reconsideration within 7 days, and the public security organ makes a decision within 30 days. If still dissatisfied, they can apply to the next level up for review within a further 7 days.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: 公安部 (2020). 公安机关办理刑事案件程序规定（公安部令第 159 号修正后，第一百七十一、一百七十八、一百七十九条）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5549884.htm>；公安部 (2013/2019 修正). 公安机关办理行政案件程序规定（公安部令第 125 号、第 149 号）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2321117.htm>
- Notes: Why this is graded C: the judgement that "many people treat the person behind the window as a role rather than as someone at work" has no literature to cite. All that can be cited is the other half, the rules: the deadlines, the paperwork and the routes of redress are all set out in writing. Every figure in this entry comes from the two Ministry of Public Security regulations in §8.39 (what happens after you call the police). Following the procedure does nothing about "they were rude to me", and it does not guarantee the matter gets resolved; all it changes is your expectations and your first step. It is the same with bank clerks and government service windows: their actions leave a record, they need a second check, and they are held to account for mistakes. So what to do is keep the slip, ask for the receipt, and complain through the channels, rather than guessing at "is he going to do me over". The lawful order of priority is all set out in the open, for example emergency departments go by how serious the condition is, not by who arrived first (§24.7, emergency triage). If you want to thank a doctor who saved you, go through a letter of thanks and the satisfaction rating, see §24.11 (letter of thanks instead of a red envelope). Wanting to "give someone a little something so they take more care" is, with people handling cases and enforcing the law, the crime of bribery, and one the law expressly treats more heavily, see §8.40 (do not give money or cards to people handling cases). If you meet obvious unequal treatment, complain if complaining is warranted, and do not copy it as though it were how things are done.

### 20. Do not make "how other people are doing" daily reading: set a limit on apps you scroll for your peers' updates, or turn them off
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Setting a limit on an app, or deactivating the account, is a few minutes' work either way. The hard part is not opening it again afterwards, which means fighting habit for the long run.
- In plain words: People in the habit of comparing themselves online with people doing better tend to feel worse and to have lower self-esteem. People who deactivated Facebook for four weeks saw a small rise in well-being, but their grasp of current affairs also fell off a fair way. People who limited each of three social platforms to 10 minutes a day had lower loneliness and depression three weeks later.
- Benefit: Pooling several studies together (a meta-analysis): 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583, using a three-level random effects model. The average correlation between online upward social comparison and poor psychological adjustment was r = 0.330 (95% CI 0.289–0.370, the confidence range). Looking at the specific outcomes separately: negative feelings about being judged socially, such as fear of being looked down on, 0.438, and anxiety 0.382. Depression 0.306, low well-being 0.268, low self-esteem 0.263. Age, cultural background and the year the data were collected all made no difference to how strong the association was. Results from studies that followed people over time were no stronger than those from surveys taken at one point in time. There is also a randomised controlled trial, with 2,743 American Facebook users as participants. 61% of them were willing to accept deactivation for less than 102 dollars. Those people were randomly split into a four-week deactivation group and a control group. Deactivation freed up 60 minutes a day for the average participant. The subjective well-being index rose by 0.09 standard deviations. Broken down, that is life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09 and happiness 0.08. After taking out the part attributable to chance across multiple comparisons, these still count. The authors give a reference point, which is psychological interventions designed to make people more positive. Interventions of that kind shift subjective well-being by about +0.34 standard deviations and depression by about -0.23 standard deviations. So this effect is about 25% to 40% of a standard psychological intervention. The cost over the same period was: the current affairs knowledge index fell by 0.19 standard deviations, and the degree of political polarisation fell by 0.16 standard deviations. After the experiment ended, the deactivation group's Facebook use was still 12 minutes/day lower than the control group's (0.31 standard deviations, a drop of 23%). There is also a randomised controlled trial with 143 undergraduates as participants, randomly split into two groups. One group limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes a day per platform, and the other used them as usual. Three weeks later, loneliness and depression had clearly fallen in the limited group. Anxiety and fear of missing out were lower than at the start in both groups.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514（the specific figures cited in this entry were checked word for word against this version, because the published version's abstract contains no figures）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514>；Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Notes: The "correlation" half only shows that two things occur together, and must not be read as one causing the other; the authors themselves say more studies following people over time are needed. The randomised half can speak to cause, but the participants were Facebook users in the United States before the 2018 midterm elections, so moving it to Chinese platforms is a stretch. The Hunt study had only 143 undergraduates, and it is the only one of its kind. None of the effect sizes are large, so do not expect turning an app off to solve an emotional problem. What this is meant to block is the "see how other people are doing" kind of use, and it does not cover keeping in touch with family and friends, looking things up, or job hunting. If you really cannot hold off, start by turning off push notifications, "people you may know", and the home feed recommendations. For total screen time, the arithmetic is in §4.15 and §4.16 (a hard limit on short videos, and not watching rolling news). For what to do first when your mood is low, see §3.18 (get moving, get sunlight, sleep on schedule). For looking for a sense of identity by buying things, see §6.23 (do not expect buying things to improve your mood). For spending more to "move up a notch", see §6.24 (do not spend more to move up a notch among the people around you).

### 21. When you feel that "everybody saw me make a fool of myself", divide that estimate by two
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You just ask yourself one more question when you are nervous: did they actually see it?
- In plain words: People who walked into a room of others in an embarrassing T-shirt thought nearly half the room would recognise the face printed on it, and in fact fewer than a quarter did. When the T-shirt was one they thought made them look good, their estimate of how many people would recognise it was six times the real number. After a conversation, people also generally underestimate how much the other person liked them. So when you feel that everybody will remember you making a fool of yourself, halving it is still too high.
- Benefit: Experiment one: participants put on a T-shirt with a picture of Barry Manilow on it and walked into a room with other participants. The wearers estimated on average that 46% of the observers could correctly name the person on the shirt. The observers were actually correct 23% of the time, so the estimate was exactly double the real figure. Subtracting the actual figure from the estimate session by session, the average overestimate was 23 percentage points (95% CI 9–38, the confidence range). Leaving out the two sessions that had only two and three observers, it was 27 percentage points (11–43). Experiment two: participants instead wore a T-shirt of their own choosing that they thought showed them in a good light. The average estimate was 48% and the observers were actually correct 8% of the time, six times over. Another series of studies used three settings: strangers paired up to talk, first-year university roommates, and an adult workshop. The result is that people systematically underestimate how much the person they are talking to likes them, and also underestimate how much the other person enjoyed the conversation. The authors call this the "liking gap". It was present in conversations of different lengths, and among first-year roommates it lasted for months.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>；Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Notes: Why this is graded B: these are all laboratory and campus studies with few people, and the settings are what you wear and chatting with strangers. Moving it to "colleagues talking about me" or "saying the wrong thing in a meeting" has no direct evidence. These figures only show the direction of the overestimate and roughly how large the factor is, and they cannot be used as a coefficient. Other people's views are not all unimportant either. Where you really are being assessed, such as a job interview, a thesis defence, an appraisal or a health check report, the assessment is real, so prepare if preparation is called for. What you should discount is only that everyday feeling that everybody is watching you, and what that part has to deal with is your own estimation bias.

### 22. Treat "other people demand that I be perfect" as a symptom, not as a fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is treating a thought like this as something to be dealt with, rather than as an accurate measurement of what other people actually demand.
- In plain words: People who constantly feel that the people around them demand perfection, and will reject them if they fall short, are more likely to have suicidal thoughts. "I demand perfection of myself" has a much weaker relationship with suicidal thoughts, only about a third as strong as the first kind. After taking out the thoughts someone already had, the first kind of feeling is still linked to having more such thoughts later, though by a small amount.
- Benefit: Pooling several studies together (a meta-analysis): 45 studies, N = 11,747, with the populations being undergraduates, medical students, community adults and psychiatric patients, using a random effects model. The weighted average correlations with suicidal thoughts are below. Perfectionistic concerns r = .28 (95% CI .24–.32, the confidence range; 42 studies, 7,936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is a composite, taking in socially prescribed perfectionism, concern over mistakes, doubts about actions, the gap between reality and one's standards, and so on. Taken on its own within that, socially prescribed perfectionism is also r = .28 (.25–.32, 30 studies, 3,640 people). Perfectionistic strivings r = .10 (.07–.13, 31 studies, 4,588 people), which is a composite of self-oriented perfectionism and personal standards. Parental criticism r = .20 (.11–.29). Now the relationship with suicide attempts. Perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07–.17, 15 studies, 5,275 people). Socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08–.29, 8 studies, 689 people). As for the part that followed people over a period: after taking out the suicidal thoughts someone had at the start, socially prescribed perfectionism still moved in the same direction as suicidal thoughts at later follow-up, by a small amount. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no visible association.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Notes: Why this is graded B: most of the studies included were one-off questionnaires, so you cannot see which came first. The correlation is medium in strength (r about .28), which accounts for roughly a few per cent up to a tenth of the variation. What was measured is suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts, not death itself, so the benefit magnitude is recorded as small on the basis that there is only a surrogate endpoint. The authors themselves say that the set covering perfectionistic concerns and suicide attempts looks like it has publication bias. Publication bias means studies with unimpressive results were never published. After adding the estimated missing studies back in, the direction of the conclusion does not change. High standards are not themselves a problem, and you do not have to lower what you ask of yourself because of this. The difference lies in where the standard comes from, and in whether falling short of it makes you feel that you as a person are finished. Like the pessimistic expectations in §3.15 (treat a thought like "things are bound to get worse" as a symptom), it is something that can be dealt with as a symptom. If you have suicidal thoughts, call 12356 first and move the means of killing yourself out of reach, see §1.25 (depression or suicidal thoughts). For what you can do when someone near you shows thoughts like this, see §8.15 (a close relative can take them in for care). For depression screening in children, see §30.8 (depression screening for children).

### 23. When you are angry, do not vent by smashing things, hitting a punchbag or going for a run; slow your body down first
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. In the heat of the moment you switch to a few minutes of something that slows your heart rate: breathe slowly, sit down, be still. The hard part is that what you most want to do at that moment is precisely to vent.
- In plain words: Hitting a punchbag, throwing things, going for a run — these ways of venting, which make the body more worked up, do not get rid of anger overall. Slow breathing, mindfulness and meditation, which calm the body down, clearly do bring down anger and the urge to lash out.
- Benefit: Pooling 154 studies together (a meta-analysis), covering 184 samples and 10,189 people. Ways of calming the body down, such as deep breathing, mindfulness and meditation, reduced anger and aggression. The effect size was g = -0.63, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.43 (the confidence range). This result was stable across people of different sexes, ethnicities, ages and cultures. Ways of making the body more worked up, such as hitting a punchbag, jogging and cycling, did not work overall. Their effect size was g = -0.02, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.09. The authors' conclusion is that venting anger and going for a run are both not effective ways of getting rid of anger.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>
- Notes: What was measured is anger and aggression, not energy. Recording the lens as time is the author's classification: one argument fewer is one mess fewer to clear up. For what you can use on the spot, see §22.8 (cyclic sighing) and also §3.17 (leave the room when you are angry). Running is not useless. Over the long run it is effective for low mood, see §22.6 (walk or run when your mood is bad). It is just not suited to getting rid of anger on the spot. In the heat of the moment, do not drive, do not send messages, and do not make decisions. If someone is hitting you, leave first and call the police. What has to be dealt with then is not your feelings, see §8.43 (when you are being beaten at home).

### 24. If something is weighing on you, you can write down your thoughts and feelings for several days in a row, but do not expect it to treat an illness
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The usual approach in the research is to write 3 to 4 times, 15 to 20 minutes each time. You write about something that upsets you, and you write your deepest thoughts and feelings.
- In plain words: Writing down a bad experience brings a small benefit on average, but a very small one. The pooled conclusions do not agree either, and two pooled analyses worked out to no effect. It costs almost nothing, so you can try it. If your emotional problems get heavy, do not use it in place of seeing a doctor.
- Benefit: One analysis pooled 146 randomised studies (a meta-analysis). The people in them wrote down or spoke about something that mattered to them. The average effect was positive and statistically significant, but very small, with an effect size of r = .075. Another pooled analysis took in only randomised controlled trials, and across 30 trials together there was no significant effect on either physical health or mental health. Pooling 16 randomised trials in cancer patients and survivors, there was no significant effect on any of psychological outcomes, physical outcomes or quality of life. The psychological side there was g = 0.04, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.14 (the confidence range).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Frattaroli J (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823>；Mogk C, Otte S, Reinhold-Hurley B, Kröner-Herwig B (2006). Health effects of expressive writing on stressful or traumatic experiences - a meta-analysis. Psycho-Social Medicine. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2736499/>；Zachariae R, O'Toole MS (2015). The effect of expressive writing intervention on psychological and physical health outcomes in cancer patients--a systematic review and meta-analysis. Psycho-Oncology. <https://doi.org/10.1002/pon.3802>
- Notes: Disputed. On the supporting side is the pooled set of 146 studies, where the effect was positive but very small. On the opposing side are two pooled analyses that took in only randomised controlled trials, neither of which came out with a significant effect. The number of sessions and their length in the cost field come from the earliest experimental design, which the abstract of the Mogk analysis states. It costs almost nothing, it does not take much time, and you can do it on your own. When you do not want to talk to anyone, or there is nobody around to talk to, you can use it. What it measured is not energy, and recording the lens as time is the author's classification. If writing makes you feel worse and worse, stop and call 12356 instead, see §1.25 (12356).

### 25. Take an ibuprofen-type painkiller for period pain rather than enduring it; if the pain stops you working or studying, or three months of medication does not control it, go to gynaecology to be checked
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A box of ibuprofen costs about ten-odd yuan, and you take it only on the days it hurts. If you need to go to gynaecology, registration and an ultrasound cost some money and half a day.
- In plain words: With an ibuprofen-type drug for period pain, the share of people whose pain clearly eases rises from under two in ten to about half. It works better than paracetamol, with somewhat more side effects. If the pain stops you working or studying, if you have deep pain during sex, or if three months of medication still does not control it, go to gynaecology to be checked for endometriosis. On average this condition takes 6.7 years to diagnose.
- Benefit: A Cochrane systematic review pooled 80 randomised controlled trials covering 5820 women with primary dysmenorrhoea. Primary dysmenorrhoea means period pain with no other condition found behind it. Compared with placebo, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs relieved pain more effectively (OR 4.37, 95% CI 3.76–5.09, the confidence range). Converted over, 18% of people on placebo got moderate or better relief, against 45% to 53% of people on these drugs. The cost is somewhat more side effects (OR 1.29, 1.11–1.51). 10% of people on placebo had side effects, against 11% to 14% on these drugs. The extra is mainly stomach and bowel upset (OR 1.58) and nervous system reactions (OR 2.74). Compared with paracetamol, these drugs also relieved pain more effectively (OR 1.89, 1.05–3.43), though on only 3 trials. Which of these drugs is best showed no difference. The British NICE guideline (updated 2024) lists the situations in which to suspect endometriosis. They are long-term pelvic pain, period pain that affects daily activities and quality of life, and deep pain during or after sex. Also painful bowel movements during periods, blood in the urine or painful urination during periods, and infertility alongside these symptoms. Girls aged 17 and under are included too. The guideline advises trying paracetamol or these drugs first, for example for 3 months. If the pain relief is not enough, refer the person on for further investigation. A normal gynaecological examination and a normal ultrasound do not rule the condition out either. One study surveyed 1418 women undergoing laparoscopy in 16 hospitals across 10 countries. From symptoms appearing to endometriosis being confirmed at surgery, the average delay was 6.7 years, with most of the delay in the primary care stage. Women who were diagnosed did 10.8 fewer hours of work a week on average, mostly as being at work with reduced output. A Dutch online survey asked 32748 women aged 15 to 45. 13.8% had taken leave because of their period, 1.3 days a year on average. 80.7% were at work during their period but with reduced output, which works out as 8.9 fewer days of work a year.
- Evidence: A

- Notes: People over 60, people who have had a stomach bleed, and people taking anticoagulants or hormones should ask a doctor before using these drugs. The detail is in §34.3 (ask a doctor first before taking an ibuprofen-type painkiller). The American ibuprofen label states that it can cause severe allergic reactions, which are more likely in people allergic to aspirin, and that an asthma attack is one of the ways this shows. People with asthma should also ask a doctor before using it. If you might be pregnant, ask a doctor first, and after 20 weeks of pregnancy do not take it on your own, see §34.5 (after 20 weeks of pregnancy). If you cannot take these drugs, you can switch to paracetamol, and the daily limit is in §34.1 (paracetamol). For period pain you only take the medicine on the days of your period. The threshold in §34.9 (do not take painkillers more than 15 days a month) is about headache, and the number of days of medication for period pain is nowhere near it. It is graded A because the pain relief comes from a large number of randomised trials. The reviewers rated the quality of evidence for most comparisons as low, mainly because the trials describe their methods unclearly, and 59% of the trials were funded by drug companies. Recording the benefit as medium is a judgement: the review only measured whether it hurt, not how many days of leave are saved. In that Dutch survey, the loss caused by periods works out as about 10 days a year, which spread over a week is in the order of hours. The group with endometriosis were people who had already gone for laparoscopy, so they do not represent most people with period pain. The beneficiary is mainly you yourself.
