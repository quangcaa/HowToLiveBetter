# 4. Do not waste time

This section counts time only. It looks at how many hours you can save back each day, each week and each year, or at how much you can raise the share of things you finish on time. It does not convert time into lifespan, and it does not convert time into money. Where money comes up, it gives you the arithmetic and not a conclusion. For most entries here the evidence is a single study, or total figures from a time-use survey, so the grades are mostly B and C.

### 1. Rewrite "I plan to do this" as "at this time, in this place, when this happens I do that"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Each item takes 1 to 2 minutes, and you write one "if ... then ..." sentence. For example: "when I sit down at my desk at 8 am on Tuesday, I write the introduction first".
- In plain words: Rewrite "I am going to do this" as "when I sit down at my desk at 8 am on Tuesday, I write the introduction first". Taking 94 separate tests together, people who write it this way reach their goal noticeably more often, and the effect is medium to large. All you change is the wording of one sentence, which costs no money and no effort.
- Benefit: Taking 94 independent tests together, people who first write their plan as "if ... then ..." reach their goals more often. Psychology calls this way of writing an implementation intention. The effect size is d = 0.65, which counts as medium to large. It works at three points: getting started, holding up when something interrupts you, and backing out of an effort that cannot succeed.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: This trick only works on something you genuinely want to do. If you do not want the goal itself, no number of sentences will help. Also, most of these studies recruited students and looked at health habits, so it may not work as well once you move it to work.

### 2. Write down your exit condition before you start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. When you start, spend 5 minutes writing out "if I have not reached this figure by this date, I stop". The hard part is owning it when the day comes, because stopping in front of other people is a bit embarrassing.
- In plain words: Before you start, write one sentence: if I have not reached this figure by this date, I stop. Taking several studies together, writing it this way does help people back out of an effort that cannot succeed. What you save is all the time you would otherwise have kept pouring in.
- Benefit: Taking several implementation intention studies together, an "if ... then ..." set in advance also helps people back out of an effort that cannot succeed. The original paper's wording is disengagement from failing courses of action. The time you save is the time you would have gone on investing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Set the exit condition right at the start, while you are still calm. If you set it later, what you have already put in will pull you off course. When you judge, look only at future effort and future payoff, see §4.3 (look only at future effort and future payoff). Also, willingness to back out is only one sub-finding inside a pooled set of studies, and nobody has worked out on its own how much time it saves.

### 3. When you decide whether to continue, look only at future effort and future payoff, not at how much you have already put in
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is accepting one thing: the time you already put in is gone, and you cannot get it back.
- In plain words: Money and time you have already put in make you want to keep going. Taking 98 sets of data together, that pull is there consistently. So when you decide whether to continue, count only how much more you have to put in and how much you can still get back. Leave out what you have already spent.
- Benefit: Money, energy and time you have already put in make you want to keep going. Psychology calls this the sunk cost effect. Pooling 98 sets of data, the analysis confirms that the pull is there consistently. If you avoid it, you stop putting more time in just because you do not want to waste what you spent.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>；Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: The Arkes and Blumer theatre experiment ran like this. A theatre at Ohio University sold season tickets, and buyers were randomly given one of three prices: the full 15 dollars, 2 dollars off, or 7 dollars off. In the first half of the season the full-price group attended 4.11 performances on average, against 3.32 and 3.29 for the other two groups. In the second half of the season there was no clear difference between the three groups. Roth and colleagues found that the effect fades over time, and that older people are affected less.

### 4. Estimate how long work will take from how long similar past tasks actually took, not from the plan
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Before you estimate, spend a few minutes looking up how long the last similar job actually took.
- In plain words: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish a thesis and actually took 55.5 days, and only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. Another group was asked to recall how long a similar job had taken before estimating, and the share finishing on time rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students predicted 33.9 days to finish a thesis and actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished on time against their own prediction. The researchers asked another group to link their past experience to the job in front of them before estimating. In that group the share finishing on time rose from 29.3% in the control group to 60.0%. The optimism that makes people estimate too short no longer showed up in that group.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>；Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>；Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: The Buehler study recruited only Canadian university students, it is a single study, and the numbers are small, 37 people and about 40 per group. Estimating from the historical data of similar projects is called reference class forecasting, and Flyvbjerg covers how engineering does it in practice. Halkjelsvik pooled several studies and points out that estimating too short is more common than estimating too long, but how far off people are varies a lot with the task and the research method.

### 5. Send an agenda before a meeting and hold no meeting without one; hold it standing up when you can
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The organiser spends 5 to 10 minutes writing an agenda beforehand. A standing meeting needs the other attendees to go along with it.
- In plain words: When an agenda goes out before a meeting, the people at it are more likely to feel the meeting was useful. In an experiment, sitting meetings ran 34% longer than standing ones, and the decisions the two formats produced were no better or worse than each other.
- Benefit: Two surveys covered 958 people and 292 people. When an agenda went out before the meeting, attendees were more likely to see the meeting as effective, and that relationship holds up statistically. In another experiment 55 groups met sitting down and 56 groups met standing up, and the sitting meetings ran 34% longer. The quality of the decisions was the same in both formats.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>；Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The standing-meeting experiment used 5-person groups doing a task in a laboratory. You cannot carry that finding straight over to long meetings or large meetings. What Leach measured is how effective attendees felt the meeting was, not how much work actually got done.

### 6. Cut the number of meetings, and hold no meeting for anything written messages people answer in their own time can settle
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You need your colleagues and your boss to go along with it. The hard part is that writing a clear paragraph takes more energy than saying one sentence out loud.
- In plain words: The more meetings you have in a day, the more tired you feel, and the heavier your own workload feels. Cut one meeting and you save exactly as long as that meeting was, with nothing shaved off.
- Benefit: Two surveys recruited employees working more than 35 hours a week, 676 people and 304 people. How much time meetings take up does not have a fixed effect on work attitudes and well-being. It depends on two things: how well the meetings are run, and how much the job depends on other people to get work done. Another study had people keep a diary for a week, and on days with more meetings, fatigue and perceived workload were higher. The time you save equals the length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>；Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes: Disputed. Rogelberg 2006 found that the link between how many meetings there are and well-being does not run one way. In jobs that depend heavily on other people, more meetings is not necessarily a bad thing. Also, no study has directly compared switching to written messages answered in each person's own time against holding the meeting, so this entry is the author's own inference.

### 7. Break a big task into subtasks before you estimate the time and before you start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. Breaking it up takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- In plain words: When people estimate how long work will take, they do not automatically break the task up and count it piece by piece. List the subtasks first and then estimate, and you underestimate by less. Once it is broken up, every small step is something you can start right away, so you put it off less easily.
- Benefit: When people estimate how long work will take, they do not split a task with several parts into pieces by themselves. Writing the literature review, writing the discussion and tidying up the references are several separate jobs to begin with. Listing the subtasks before estimating reduces the error of estimating too short, and that error is called the planning fallacy. Once it is broken up, each small step becomes an action you can start immediately, and procrastination drops with it. In the research, the factors that best predict procrastination include how unpleasant you find the task, and the task itself being pushed back.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>；Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: How far to break it up: the rule is that each subtask can be finished in one sitting, because breaking it up too finely eats time of its own. Also, for the Kruger and Evans study only the direction was verified from the abstract, and the exact percentages were not checked against the full text.

### 8. Set a date yourself for anything that has no deadline from outside
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is honouring the date once you set it, because if you shrug it off on the day, you may as well not have set it.
- In plain words: When someone else set the deadline, 80.6% of students finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within the time they predicted themselves. When the work gets done is almost entirely decided by the deadline, and barely by their own estimate. So for anything with no deadline, set one yourself and then honour it.
- Benefit: Of students with an external deadline, 80.6% finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within the time they predicted themselves. When the work got done tracked the deadline almost exactly, with a correlation coefficient r = 0.82, and the closer that number is to 1 the tighter the tracking. It had only a weak relationship with their own prediction.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: If you tell someone else about the date you set, it becomes closer to the kind someone else sets. To be clear, what the research tested is that deadlines set by other people work. Nobody has directly measured the kind you set for yourself, such as timeboxing or a pomodoro timer, so that part is inferred here.

### 9. Treat procrastination as dodging something that feels bad, not as not trying hard enough
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. All you change is where you look first: ask what about this task feels bad to you, then work out what to do.
- In plain words: Procrastination has little to do with laziness or with personality. What best shows whether someone will put a task off is how unpleasant the task feels to them, whether they believe they can pull it off, and how easily whatever is in front of them pulls them away. So what helps is making the task less unpleasant and making the first step smaller, not telling yourself off and then forcing it.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlation coefficients found several things strongly and consistently linked to procrastination: how unpleasant the task is, how far away the deadline is, self-efficacy (believing you can pull it off), impulsiveness, and conscientiousness together with the self-control, distractibility, orderliness and achievement motivation under it. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking had only weak links to procrastination. The author explains this set of results with temporal motivation theory: how attractive something is right now drops off fast as the reward moves further away.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: The benefit is recorded as medium, because these studies only point out a direction and never measured how many hours you save. The concrete actions are in §4.1 (write it as "at this time, in this place, when this happens I do that"), §4.7 (break a big task into subtasks) and §4.8 (set a date for anything with no deadline from outside). These are pooled correlational studies, so you cannot tell what causes what.

### 10. Put what you need within reach and move what you do not want to touch further away, rather than counting on resisting in the moment
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing, and takes a few minutes once. Put your phone in another room, and leave your book and your laptop open on the desk.
- In plain words: The further away something is, the less people take. Put the same snack further away and the amount people eat drops clearly, which is a medium-sized effect. Put out fewer options and clearly fewer people take it. None of this asks you to resist in the moment, and you can set it up in a few minutes.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review that included 24 randomised trials. Putting food further away reduced how much people ate, with a standardised mean difference of −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36, this is the confidence range; 12 studies, 1098 people, low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the number of options cut selection of that food more sharply, with a standardised mean difference of −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). A regression analysis showed that the further away it is and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: Why this is graded B: all 24 studies the review took in were about food, with not one about alcohol or tobacco, and 14 were done in a laboratory, all from high-income countries. So carrying "move it further away and you touch it less" over to phones and games is an extension, with no trial testing it directly. Putting your phone out of sight has been measured directly, see §3.1 (turn off non-essential notifications and keep your phone out of sight while working).

### 11. Put money on the line to make yourself follow through, but first ask yourself whether you are willing to put it up
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A deposit-style scheme means handing over your own money first, and you forfeit it if you do not follow through. In the trial the deposit was 150 dollars.
- In plain words: To push yourself to follow through, you can take a bonus once you succeed, or hand over a deposit first that you get back plus a bonus only if you succeed. Among people willing to join either way, 13.2 percentage points more of the deposit group followed through. But only 13.7% were willing to pay a deposit, while 90% were willing to take a bonus, so a deposit only helps people who are willing to put money up.
- Benefit: A randomised trial in the United States with 2538 people assigned employees and their friends and family at random to four incentive schemes or to usual care. Acceptance was 90.0% for the reward type (about 800 dollars if you succeed) and 13.7% for the deposit type (150 dollars up front, plus a 650 dollar reward). Sustained abstinence from smoking at six months was between 9.4% and 16.0% across the four incentive schemes, against 6.0% for usual care. The reward type was 15.7% and the deposit type 10.2%. But counting only the 13.7% who were willing to join either way, the deposit type's six-month quit rate was 13.2 percentage points higher than the reward type's (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, this is the confidence range).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: This trial tested quitting smoking, so carrying it over to things like writing a thesis or working out is an extension, which is why the benefit is recorded as medium. When you pick a scheme, first look at whether you will actually use it. A method most people refuse does nothing for those people, however strong its effect. So pick the kind you are willing to accept, because a small deposit still beats no deposit. For how to quit smoking itself, see §2.3 (stop-smoking medication).

### 12. Count a new habit in months, not in "21 days"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. What it takes is repeating the same action every day in the same situation, for several months.
- In plain words: "It takes 21 days to form a habit" has nothing behind it. One study that followed people for 12 weeks worked out that an action becomes something you do without thinking in 18 days at the fast end and 254 days at the slow end. Two or three weeks in, it is normal for it still not to feel easier. Missing a day along the way does not reset your progress to zero.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each picked one eating, drinking or activity action and did it every day in a fixed situation (for example "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, scoring how automatic it felt each day. Data from 82 people were usable for analysis, and the model fitted successfully for 62 people. The number of days needed to reach 95% of each person's own automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 days to 254 days. The study also found that missing one chance to perform the action had no real effect on the habit forming.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: There is only this one study, with 96 people, and all the actions were simple ones, such as eating a piece of fruit after a meal. How long complex things take, such as running three times a week or writing every day, nobody has measured. So if it still feels hard after two weeks, that is not evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, tie the action to one fixed situation, see §4.1 (write it as "at this time, in this place, when this happens I do that").

### 13. If procrastination is already affecting your life, use self-help material backed by a randomised trial, and you do not have to find a therapist
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A self-help book costs a few tens of yuan, and some are free. It takes about ten weeks, reading one section and doing one exercise each week.
- In plain words: Procrastination bad enough to cause you distress can be treated. Working through cognitive behavioural self-help material for ten weeks clearly beat doing nothing, both with someone guiding you and entirely on your own. No difference showed up between having a guide and not having one, so working through it yourself works too.
- Benefit: 150 people were split at random into three groups: self-help with therapist guidance, self-help without guidance, and a waiting-list control. After ten weeks, the between-group effect size on the Pure Procrastination Scale was d = 0.70 for the guided group (95% CI 0.29–1.10, this is the confidence range) and d = 0.50 for the unguided group (0.10–0.90). On the Irrational Procrastination Scale the figures were 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09). The share reaching clinically meaningful improvement was 31.3% to 40.0% in the guided group and 24.0% to 36.0% in the unguided group. The two groups did not differ significantly on any outcome.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: The participants were Swedish adults using Swedish-language material, and there is no ready-made equivalent in Chinese, which is why the benefit is recorded as medium. The ingredients in the material are the usual few: behavioural activation, behavioural experiments, stimulus control, and teaching about motivation and ways of working. Stimulus control is the one in §4.10 (move what you do not want to touch further away). If your procrastination comes with clear low mood or anxiety, start with §3.18 (get moving, get sunlight, sleep on schedule, talk to someone, call 12356).

### 14. Use your own hourly wage to decide which chores to outsource
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: You pay money for the outsourcing, and how much depends on which service you buy. You also spend time once, working out your own hourly wage.
- In plain words: People in China spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework. Among the people who actually did some that day, it is 1 hour 59 minutes. People who spend money to buy time are more satisfied with their lives. The same money makes you happier in the moment when you spend it on buying time than on buying things.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework. Counting only the people who did housework, the average is 1 hour 59 minutes. This is a 2024 survey covering 38500 households and 107000 people. Another study took a sample of 6271 people in four countries, and people who spent money to buy time were more satisfied with their lives. The researchers also ran an experiment in real life: the same money brought higher happiness in the moment when spent on buying time than on buying physical goods.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>；国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: Work out your hourly wage first, by taking one month's after-tax income ÷ the hours you actually worked that month. Then find out what outsourcing that chore costs. If the price is below your hourly wage, and you really will use the time saved to earn money or to rest, outsource it. Everyone's hourly wage and local prices differ, so you have to do the arithmetic yourself. Also, what Whillans measured is whether people were happier after buying time, not how many hours they saved.

### 15. Set a hard limit on short videos and aimless scrolling
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The action takes only a few minutes: turn off the push notifications, delete the app, or set a daily time limit on the app. The hard part is keeping your hands off it and not opening it.
- In plain words: People in China spend an average of 5 hours 37 minutes a day online. Among the people who actually went online that day it is 6 hours 3 minutes, while in 2018 the same calculation gave only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest number in this section. Cut one hour a day and you gain 365 hours in a year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 5 hours 37 minutes a day online. Counting only the people who actually went online that day, the average is 6 hours 3 minutes. People who used the internet that day were 92.9%. These are 2024 figures. In 2018 the same calculation gave only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest number in this section. Cutting one hour a day out of it means 365 more hours a year. Separately, the China Internet Network Information Center reports that by June 2025 internet users were online an average of 30.6 hours a week, 1.9 hours more than half a year earlier. Short-video users numbered 1.068 billion people, 95.1% of internet users.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: 国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>；中国互联网络信息中心 (2025). 第 56 次《中国互联网络发展状况统计报告》. <https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2025/0730/MAIN1753846666507QEK67ZS9DH.pdf>
- Notes: Time online includes work, study and keeping in touch with people, so it is not all waste. It is graded C because no study has worked out how much time a limit saves back, or where that time then went, and all we have is total figures. For one part of it, scrolling to see how other people are doing, a randomised trial has measured how much time comes back and how much mood changes, see §3.20 (setting a limit on apps that show how other people are doing).

### 16. Skip television and rolling news, and read the information you need at set times in one batch
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. The hard part is breaking the habit of opening it without thinking, and you may also miss some news as it breaks.
- In plain words: People aged 15 and over in the United States watch 2.6 hours of television a day, which takes up half of all their leisure time. People in China watch an average of 1 hour 40 minutes a day, and people aged 75 to 84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes. If you switch to reading what you need at set times in one batch, what you save is that whole block.
- Benefit: People aged 15 and over in the United States watch 2.6 hours of television a day. Their total leisure time is 5.2 hours, so television takes up half of it. These are 2025 figures. Chinese residents watch television an average of 1 hour 40 minutes a day, and people aged 75 to 84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes. These are 2018 figures.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Notes: All we have is total figures. No study has worked out where the saved time goes once people watch less television and read less news. Reading it in one batch is the author's own experience.

### 17. Spend time once learning the keyboard shortcuts and automation in the software you use most
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. You put in a few hours to a few dozen hours once to learn it. The hard part is that for a while at the start you are slower than with the mouse.
- In plain words: Most people who have used Word for years still click the toolbar icons. In an experiment comparing groups, shortcuts were faster than both menus and icons. Learning it is a one-off cost, and after that every action every day earns it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more you earn back.
- Benefit: The research looked at 251 experienced Word users, and most of them rarely used the faster shortcuts and mainly clicked the icon toolbar. An experiment comparing groups confirmed that of the three ways, menus, icons and keyboard shortcuts, shortcuts are the fastest. The time you get back builds up bit by bit with the number of actions you do each day. People who do the same kind of work over a long time gain the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Notes: Only learn the actions you use more than ten times a day, because learning the whole set is a waste of another kind. To be clear, that efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract does not give the seconds saved per action.

### 18. Put the length of the commute near the front when you choose where to live, and shorten the one-way commute
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: The cost is high. You may have to pay higher rent, or move to a smaller place. Moving itself takes time too.
- In plain words: The longer people commute, the worse they feel their own life is going. That loss is not made up for in pay or in housing. Cut 30 minutes off the one-way trip and you save 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend an average of 50 minutes a day travelling. Counting only the people who went out that day, it is 1 hour 2 minutes. These are 2024 figures. German data that followed the same people over many years shows that the longer the commute, the lower people's subjective well-being (how well they feel their own life is going), consistently. That loss is also not compensated in income or in housing. A review that pulled several studies together confirms that the longer the commute time, the less satisfied people are with the commute, and this holds whatever mode of transport they use. Cutting 30 minutes off the one-way trip saves about 5 hours a week.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>；Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>；国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: Disputed. Chatterjee and colleagues pooled several studies and point out that no steady relationship is yet visible between commute length and overall life satisfaction. They hold that people broadly manage to trade the loss from a long commute back through a better job or better housing. Note also that the travel statistics count all trips, not commuting alone. And if you can travel outside the rush hour, the same route also takes less time.
