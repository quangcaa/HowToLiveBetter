# 14. Account and information security

Lens: money and personal information. When someone else logs into your account, the first thing you lose is money. They will also use your account to scam the people in your contacts. And your identity has been taken from you as well.

### 1. Turn on two-factor verification for your email, payment and social accounts; prefer a pop-up prompt on your phone, and only then an SMS code
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. You set it once per account, and it takes two or three minutes.
- In plain words: Two-factor verification means that when you log in, you confirm once more that it is really you, on top of the password. With the method where a window pops up on your phone and you tap to confirm, more than nine in ten phishing takeovers get blocked. With the old method of answering "where did you log in last time" or "what is your backup email", only about one in ten phishing takeovers get blocked.
- Benefit: Google counted 350000 real account takeover attempts (also called account hijacking). One kind of challenge verifies you through a device, such as a window popping up on your phone for you to tap, or a security key you plug in. This kind blocked "over 94% of phishing-based hijacking attempts and 100% of automated hijacking attempts". Phishing means someone tricks you into typing your password on a fake site. Automated means a machine tries leaked passwords in bulk. The other kind verifies you by asking questions, such as where you logged in last time or what your backup email is. This kind "blocked as little as 10% of phishing hijackings and 73% of automated hijackings"
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Doerfler P, Thomas K, Marincenko M, et al. (2019). Evaluating Login Challenges as a Defense Against Account Takeover. The World Wide Web Conference (WWW '19). <https://doi.org/10.1145/3308558.3313481>
- Notes: The same study also found that these challenges sometimes lock the real owner out. 52% of genuine users did not get in on the first try, but 97% did get in in the end. Turn this on for your email first, because most other accounts can have their password reset through email

### 2. Give your email its own password, not one you reuse on any website
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. If a password manager stores it, you do not have to remember it. The hard part is breaking the old habit of using one password everywhere.
- In plain words: A password stolen from another website gets used to log straight into your email. Once someone is inside your email, every account whose password can be reset through that email goes with it. So give your email a password of its own, and use it nowhere else.
- Benefit: Taking account names and passwords leaked elsewhere and trying them one by one is called credential stuffing, and it is the laziest kind of attack. Your email will be tried this way too. Once someone is inside your email, every account that resets its password through it goes with it. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advises this: use a different strong password for every account, at least 16 characters, and let a password manager store it
- Evidence: C
- Sources: US CISA. Use Strong Passwords. <https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords>
- Notes: If you cannot remember them, use the password manager built into your browser. It remembers each site's password for you, which is far better than using the same password everywhere. Do not keep passwords in WeChat Favorites or in a notes app

### 3. Set a lock screen passcode on your phone, and a PIN on your SIM card
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. You set the lock screen passcode once and the PIN once.
- In plain words: The SIM card is the small card inside your phone, and it is what receives SMS verification codes. If you lose the phone, whoever picks it up can pull that card out, put it in another phone to receive your codes, and then reset your accounts one by one. Set a PIN on the card, and once the card is in another phone it asks for that code at startup, so this route is closed.
- Benefit: After you lose your phone, the fastest route for whoever picks it up is to put the SIM card in another phone and receive your SMS verification codes. With the codes, he can reset your accounts one by one. If the SIM card has a PIN, it asks for the code at startup in another phone, so whoever picked it up cannot use it to receive codes
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience, no direct literature
- Notes: You set the PIN under the "SIM card lock" item in your phone settings. The factory default code is usually 1234 or 0000. If you enter it wrong three times in a row, you need the PUK code from your carrier to unlock it. So write your code down on paper as soon as you have set it

### 4. If you lose your phone, do it in this order: report the SIM card lost, lock the phone remotely, change passwords, call the police, freeze the bank card
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. The whole set takes a bit over ten minutes.
- In plain words: The order matters more than how fast your hands are. First report the SIM card lost, which cuts off the verification codes. Then lock the phone remotely, change your email and payment passwords from a computer, call the police and get a receipt, and freeze the bank card if needed. With the phone gone, you can still report the card lost by borrowing someone else's phone to call your carrier.
- Benefit: The order matters more than speed. Step one, report the SIM card lost, so nobody else can receive your verification codes. Step two, lock the phone remotely and wipe what is on it. Step three, change your email and payment passwords from a computer. Step four, call the police and get a receipt. Last, freeze the bank card if you need to. The US Federal Communications Commission says the same: lock it remotely even if you think you only misplaced it. If it was stolen, call the police at once, give the model and the IMEI number (the phone's identity number), and tell your carrier right away
- Evidence: C
- Sources: US FCC. Protect Your Smart Device. <https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/protect-your-mobile-device>；the order of the steps is the author's experience；to replace a lost ID card see §7.11 (replace a lost ID card at once), for a loan taken out in your name see §8.9 (check your own credit report twice a year, free)
- Notes: Write down the three carriers' service numbers in advance: China Mobile 10086, China Unicom 10010, China Telecom 10000. Note which city you got your own number in too, because the service line will ask. You can call the service line from someone else's phone to report the loss just as well

### 5. If your card is used fraudulently, report it lost and freeze it first, then call the police, then demand the bank pay: proving "you made the transaction yourself" is the bank's job
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. The moment you see something wrong on the card, report it lost or freeze it. Keep the police record, the lost-card record and the transaction notices the bank sent. If the card is still on you, make a small balance check or a small deposit or withdrawal at the nearest branch, so the record shows the real card was in your hands when it happened. The hard part is holding back from arguing with the service line first, and reporting the loss first.
- In plain words: If someone uses your card fraudulently, you do not have to prove "I did not make this transaction". The other way round, the bank has to produce evidence that you made it yourself, and if it cannot, it has to pay you. This holds only if you report the card lost and freeze it as soon as you notice. If you put off reporting it, the extra losses after that are yours to bear.
- Benefit: The Supreme People's Court rules split up who has to produce the evidence. If you say this was a counterfeit-card transaction or an online fraudulent transaction, you produce evidence first. A counterfeit-card transaction means someone copied your card and used the copy. Any of these can serve as proof: a legal document already in force, where the real card was at the time of the card transaction, and where the transaction took place. Also account transaction records, transaction notices, the police record, the lost-card record and so on. **The other way round, if the issuing bank or a non-bank payment institution (third-party payment) says the cardholder made the transaction himself, or that it was made with his authorisation, they are the ones who have to produce the evidence**. After you have told the bank, if the bank does not verify in time, or does not provide and keep the transaction slips and the surveillance video in time, and the evidence cannot be obtained because of that, the bank bears the consequence of the missing evidence. Once this is established: a debit card (savings card) holder can require the issuing bank to pay you the principal and interest of the deposits taken, and to compensate your losses. A credit card holder can require the overdraft principal, interest and penalty that were taken to be returned, and losses compensated; if the bank in turn demands that you repay that overdraft, the court will not support it. You can also require the issuing bank to remove the corresponding bad credit record promptly (nationwide, in force from 25 May 2021)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: 最高人民法院 (2021). 关于审理银行卡民事纠纷案件若干问题的规定（第四、五、七、十四、十五条）. <https://www.court.gov.cn/fabu/xiangqing/304771.html>
- Notes: Two situations leave you bearing the loss yourself. One is that you did not look after the card, the password or the verification codes, so the fault is partly yours (the original wording is 「未尽妥善保管义务具有过错」, failing the duty to safeguard them properly and being at fault), and you bear as much as the fault. So do not tell anyone your password, and do not forward verification codes to anyone (see §14.1 (two-factor verification, prefer a pop-up prompt on your phone)). The other is that you did not report the loss in time and let the losses keep growing, so the extra part is yours. The first step is therefore always to report the loss and freeze the card, not to call the service line and argue. These rules apply to third-party payment institutions too. If one of them says 「先行赔付」 (it pays out first) in its own promotional material, and the promise is specific and clear, you can hold it to that and require it to pay first. If you were tricked into transferring the money yourself, that takes a different route; see §8.2 (if you find you have been scammed, call 110 or 96110 at once and ask for a payment stop).

### 6. Check your accounts' logged-in devices and authorised apps every so often, and clear out the ones you do not use
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. Each check takes a few minutes. The hard part is that nobody reminds you, so you have to remember to look.
- In plain words: "Logged-in devices" means the phones and computers that can still use your account right now. Someone who has stolen an account often sits quiet for a while before acting. If a device you do not recognise shows up in the list, or software you stopped using long ago is still connected to your account, log out of all sessions and then change the password.
- Benefit: A stolen account is often not used on the spot, because the other side sits quiet for a while first. The logged-in device list in an account records the phones and computers that can still use that account. The authorised app list records other companies' software that you allowed to log in with that account. An unfamiliar device in the list, and third-party software you stopped using long ago, are the easiest traces to spot
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience, no direct literature
- Notes: WeChat, Alipay, email, Apple accounts and Android accounts all have this page. If you find a device you do not recognise, tap log out of all sessions and then change the password

### 7. Do not tap "agree to all" just to use an app: if the information is not necessary, your refusal is no ground to deny you the service
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is holding back from tapping "agree to all".
- In plain words: When an app asks for your information, and it is not something the service must have, it cannot stop you using it because you refused. What it may collect is limited to what it actually needs. A map needs your location; a flashlight app asking for your contacts does not.
- Benefit: The law spells out two things. One, a company may not refuse to provide a product or service on the ground that you did not consent to the handling of your information, or that you withdrew consent; the exception is where handling that information is necessary to provide the service. Two, collection must be limited to the smallest scope needed to achieve the purpose of handling, so it may only collect what it actually needs
- Evidence: A
- Sources: 全国人大常委会 (2021). 个人信息保护法. 中国人大网. <http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202108/t20210820_313088.html>：第六条「收集个人信息，应当限于实现处理目的的最小范围，不得过度收集个人信息」；第十六条「个人信息处理者不得以个人不同意处理其个人信息或者撤回同意为由，拒绝提供产品或者服务；处理个人信息属于提供产品或者服务所必需的除外」；第十五条「基于个人同意处理个人信息的，个人有权撤回其同意。个人信息处理者应当提供便捷的撤回同意的方式」
- Notes: The test is whether this piece of information is necessary to provide this service. A map needing your location is necessary; a flashlight needing your contacts is not. Right after you install an app, go to the app permissions page in your phone settings and turn off the permissions that are not necessary. When one is actually needed, choose allow only this time.

### 8. You have the right to access, copy, correct and delete your own personal information, and you can sue if you are refused
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: No money. Only when the company drags its feet do you need to complain or sue. A real lawsuit starts at several months, and you pay your own lawyer, so complaining first is the better deal. The hard part is having to chase them again and again while they stall.
- In plain words: You can require a company to let you access, copy, correct and delete your own information. If the service has stopped, the retention period is up, or you withdrew consent, the company should delete it on its own anyway. If it refuses you, it has to give you a reason. If it does nothing, you can take it straight to court. Closing an account and deleting information are two different things, so ask for deletion separately after you close the account.
- Benefit: There are several situations where a company must delete on its own initiative: the service has stopped, the agreed retention period is up, you withdrew consent, the purpose it collected the information for has already been achieved, and so on. If it has not deleted, you can require it to delete. If it refuses to let you exercise these rights, it has to explain why. You can sue in court directly
- Evidence: A
- Sources: 全国人大常委会 (2021). 个人信息保护法. 中国人大网. <http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202108/t20210820_313088.html>：第四十五条「个人有权向个人信息处理者查阅、复制其个人信息……个人请求查阅、复制其个人信息的，个人信息处理者应当及时提供」；第四十六条更正、补充权；第四十七条列了五种应当主动删除的情形，含「（一）处理目的已实现、无法实现或者为实现处理目的不再必要」「（二）个人信息处理者停止提供产品或者服务，或者保存期限已届满」「（三）个人撤回同意」，「个人信息处理者未删除的，个人有权请求删除」；第五十条「个人信息处理者应当建立便捷的个人行使权利的申请受理和处理机制。拒绝个人行使权利的请求的，应当说明理由」「个人可以依法向人民法院提起诉讼」
- Notes: Closing an account and deleting personal information are two different things, so make a separate deletion request after you close it. Before you switch phones or sell an old phone, log out of every account on the old phone and unlink them, then do a factory reset. What the law gives you is a right to delete after the fact, and it cannot pull back for you what has already leaked out.

### 9. Face scanning is not something you must agree to: if another method exists, they cannot make you scan your face as the only option, and if you refuse they have to give you another way
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=自由 -->
- Cost: No money. When you are asked to scan your face, ask one question: is there another way to verify. If they say there is none, require them to provide one. The hard part is speaking up in person.
- In plain words: As long as another method can get the same thing done, they cannot make face scanning your only option. If you do not agree to scan your face, they have to give you a card swipe, a password, an ID card or another method like that, and they may not pressure you with "then we cannot process this". In hotel rooms, public baths, changing rooms and toilets, nobody at all may install face recognition equipment.
- Benefit: The Security Management Measures for the Application of Face Recognition Technology state: "Where another non-face-recognition method exists that achieves the same purpose or meets the same business requirement, face recognition technology shall not be used as the sole verification method. Where an individual does not consent to identity verification through face information, another reasonable and convenient method shall be provided." The Measures also state: "No organisation or individual may, on grounds such as processing business or improving service quality, mislead, defraud or coerce an individual into accepting identity verification by face recognition technology." If face information is handled on the basis of your consent, the handler has to obtain "separate consent given voluntarily and explicitly on a fully informed basis": they ask you about this one thing on its own, and only your separate yes counts. You have the right to withdraw consent, and the handler must provide a convenient way to withdraw it. Handling the face information of a minor under 14 years of age requires the consent of a parent or other guardian. Installing face recognition equipment in a public place "shall be necessary for safeguarding public security", and a conspicuous notice sign must be put up. Inside private spaces within public places, such as hotel rooms, public baths, public changing rooms and public toilets, no organisation or individual may install it. Face information shall be stored inside the face recognition device, and shall not be transmitted outward over the internet. There are two exceptions: laws or administrative regulations provide otherwise, or separate consent has been obtained (nationwide, in force from 1 June 2025)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: 国家互联网信息办公室、公安部 (2025). 人脸识别技术应用安全管理办法（第 19 号令，第十条、十二条、十三条，2025 年 6 月 1 日起施行）. <https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/21/c_1744174262156096.htm>
- Notes: What you run into most often is a residential compound gate, a rental platform, a gym or a hotel asking to record your face. When they say the system only supports face scanning, read them the wording of the Measures. The wording is: "Where another non-face-recognition method exists that achieves the same purpose or meets the same business requirement, face recognition technology shall not be used as the sole verification method." Then require them to provide a card swipe, a password, an ID card or another method like that. If they still will not, report it to your local cyberspace administration department. Where the state has separate rules for verifying identity by face, in some financial and government-service situations for instance, those rules apply. The biggest difference between a face and a password is that you cannot change a face once it has leaked, so it deserves more caution than a password. An organisation that holds the face information of 100000 people or more has to file with a cyberspace administration department at provincial level or above within 30 working days, and asking about that is one way to judge whether the other side is above board. For the right to access, correct and delete your own personal information, see §14.8 (access, correct and delete your own personal information).
